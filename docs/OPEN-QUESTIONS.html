Open questions

What is not settled. It is here to be seen, not to pad anything out.

The short pose blocks

Each opponent's pose table alternates: the even entries point at a long block that carries a figure — its height, its width and its tiles, compressed — and the odd ones at a block of exactly seven bytes that carries no figure at all. Seven bytes is four hit boxes, a column and a row, and one byte left over. The reading of that last byte is not settled.

The consequence is measurable. Of thirteen name-table dumps taken from a real machine, ten reproduce a pose computed from the ROM tile for tile. The other three show figures built from tiles the long blocks never name — 0x27, 0x30, 0x35 to 0x3A — so something else is placing them, and the short blocks are the obvious suspect.

Which of the two builds came first

What is known is measured: they share 97.0% of their instructions, differ in five constants and in two masks the hard build makes variable, and only the hard one carries Konami's hidden mark, while the easy one has the title in Latin ASCII in that same gap.

What is not known is what any of that means. The binary says what the code does, not who published it or in what order. Venturing that one is original and the other a modification, or the other way round, would be a guess, and there is nothing inside the cartridge to hold it up.

What exactly 0xE056 is

It turns up in six places as the index of four-entry tables: the random generator's seeds (0x52C5), the per-opponent thresholds (0x4729 and 0x743A), the delays (0x746B and 0x7592) and the hard build's variable mask. Everything points to it being the difficulty level or the lap, but where it gets written with a value that would confirm it has not been found.

It is the same trap that has cost dear in other disassemblies: knowing who reads a variable is not enough; you have to see who writes it and with what.

How many poses each opponent has

The five pose tables have eighteen entries — twenty for the fifth — and the blocks they point at alternate long-short: the even indices give one of between eleven and twenty-seven bytes and the odd ones give seven flat. Nine pairs, with the last block eating whatever is left.

What each pair is exactly — a pose and its variant, or the body and the weapon — is not settled. It needs all of them drawn and looked at.

The fifteen bytes at 0x595A that nothing reads

The block at 0x595A is twenty bytes: five sprite attribute entries, all five E0 00 00 00. But the ldir at 0x594C copies only five of those twenty, and that is enough, because 0x5942 has just zeroed the 0x80 destination bytes.

apunta_a.py confirms that no other address in the cartridge falls inside the block. So the last fifteen are read by nothing. Whether they are leftovers from an earlier version or room set aside on purpose, the binary does not say.

The two bytes at 0x564C

Two bytes, 00 76, between the jp at 0x5649 and the script at 0x564E. No pointer anywhere in the ROM falls in them and the script does not start there, because 0x5635 loads 0x564E. They are declared as leftovers, which is what the measurement supports.

What the tiles at 0x6C15 and 0x6C1A draw

Ten tiles in two runs of five — 0x67, 0x64, 0x65, 0x66, 0x63 and 0x6B, 0x68, 0x69, 0x6A, 0x63 — that 0x6BA5 uses to draw the energy bar depending on whether there is a lot left or little. When each run is used is known; what each tile draws exactly can be seen, but has not been tied to a meaning.

Yie Ar Kung-Fu was published by Konami for the MSX; its catalogue number is RC-725 and it is 16 KB. All rights in the game remain with their holders. This is preservation, study and documentation work, and the cartridge image is not distributed.
